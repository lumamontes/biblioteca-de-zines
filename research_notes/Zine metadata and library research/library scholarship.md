# Zine metadata and library scholarship

Research date: 2026-09-24

## Scope and method

This is a focused review of scholarship and professional practice concerning the
description, cataloguing, access, and care of zines and alternative
publications. It emphasizes peer-reviewed articles, institutional repositories,
library technical/procedural documents, official collection pages, and guidance
produced by zine librarians. The review is concerned with the tension between
standard bibliographic metadata and zine-specific practice, especially:

- creator anonymity, pseudonyms, collectives, and contextual roles;
- community language and creator autonomy;
- subject versus genre/form;
- physical description and the work/expression/manifestation boundary;
- place and local context;
- holdings and the distinction between publication and copy/file;
- rights, access, and privacy.

All examples in this note are synthetic. They are illustrations of modelling
choices, not records copied from the sources. Source limitations are stated
throughout. This note complements the repository's separate [ZineCore2
research](zinecore.md), which evaluates a contemporary machine-readable zine
profile.

## Executive synthesis

The literature does not support a simple choice between conventional standards
and unconstrained local description. The more defensible pattern is a layered
catalogue:

1. Use a stable bibliographic core for title, publication identity, dates,
   format, language, relationships, and holdings so records can be exchanged
   and discovered.
2. Preserve the publication's own language and visible evidence, including
   self-description, pseudonym, collective name, issue statement, local terms,
   unusual assembly, and creator-provided context.
3. Attach provenance, uncertainty, consent, visibility, and rights decisions to
   assertions rather than forcing them into a single public string.
4. Separate the publication concept from a repository holding, a physical copy,
   and a digital file or derivative.
5. Treat description as an ethical intervention. A more searchable record can
   also expose a person, deadname, location, sensitive subject, or copyrighted
   scan against the creator's wishes.

The central tension is therefore not standards versus community practice. It is
interoperable description versus accountable description. Standards are useful
when treated as a public exchange layer, not as permission to normalize away
the social and material conditions of zine-making.

## Source map

### Peer-reviewed and scholarly sources

**Allison Jai O'Dell, "RDA and the Description of Zines: Metadata Needs for
Alternative Publications," Journal of Library Metadata 14, no. 3-4 (2014):
255-280. DOI: [10.1080/19386389.2014.978235](https://doi.org/10.1080/19386389.2014.978235).**

The abstract identifies the article's comparison of zine-user needs with RDA,
and reports that RDA is usable but needs expansion around makers, intellectual
property, privacy, subject and genre analysis, object cataloguing, companion
standards, external vocabularies, and blurred work/expression/manifestation
boundaries. It also reports experiments in MARC and ZineCore and argues that a
linked-data model could help implementation.

**Limitations:** This is a paywalled journal article in the source consulted;
the review here relies on the publisher metadata and abstract, not a complete
reading of the article. Its 2014 discussion predates current linked-data
implementations and later community practice. Treat its recommendations as a
scholarly analysis to test, not as a current policy.

**Heidy Berthoud, "My Life as a 'Like-Minded Misfit,' or, Experiences in Zine
Librarianship," Serials Review 44, no. 1 (2018): 4-12. DOI:
[10.1080/00987913.2018.1434857](https://doi.org/10.1080/00987913.2018.1434857).**

The abstract frames zines as DIY publications outside academic and commercial
publishing conventions, especially irregular frequency, authorship, and
content, and discusses how professional practice addresses those challenges.
The article is useful as practitioner scholarship: the problem is not only
technical description, but the mismatch between institutional assumptions and
the social form of zines.

**Limitations:** The abstract is available, but the full article was not freely
available through the source checked. This is a reflective practice article,
not a controlled study or a normative standard. It should not be generalized to
all zine communities or regions.

**Jenna Freedman and Rhonda Kauffman, "Cutter and Paste: A DIY Guide for
Catalogers Who Don't Know About Zines and Zine Librarians Who Don't Know About
Cataloging," in *Library and Information Studies in Social Justice Movements
Beyond the Margins* (2013), pp. 221-246. DOI:
[10.7916/D8K35RQR](https://doi.org/10.7916/D8K35RQR). Columbia Academic
Commons repository: [record](https://academiccommons.columbia.edu/doi/10.7916/D8K35RQR).**

This open repository item is explicitly a bridge between cataloging expertise
and zine-community expertise. Its premise supports a practical division of
labor: people who know the collection and its communities should be able to
participate in the cataloguing rather than handing description entirely to
detached specialists. The guide's existence is evidence that zine cataloguing
is a community practice as well as a standards problem.

**Limitations:** The repository record exposes the chapter as an item in a
larger edited volume and the landing/download path was not consistently
machine-readable during this review. Verify pagination and any detailed
field-level recommendation against the deposited text before treating it as a
policy citation.

**Felipe de Jesus Rodrigues da Silva, *Catalogacao de publicacoes alternativas:
um estudo sobre o fanzine e o cordel* (2019), Universidade de Brasilia
undergraduate thesis. [Institutional repository record](https://bdm.unb.br/handle/10483/22374).**

This source is particularly relevant to Biblioteca because it addresses
alternative publications in a Brazilian research context and compares fanzine
and cordel cataloguing. It should be consulted for Portuguese-language
terminology and local practice rather than assuming that English-language US
zine-library guidance transfers directly.

**Limitations:** This is an undergraduate thesis, not a peer-reviewed journal
article or an official cataloguing code. The repository record was used as the
authoritative citation; detailed claims should be checked against the full
Portuguese text. It may reflect the scope and standards available in 2019 and
should not be treated as a complete account of Brazilian alternative-publication
cataloguing.

### Zine-community and professional guidance

**Zine Librarians Code of Ethics, November 2015 edition.**
[Web version](https://www.zinelibraries.info/code-of-ethics/) and [PDF
edition](https://www.zinelibraries.info/wp-content/uploads/2015/11/EthicsZine-rev-20151105.pdf).

This is a collaboratively produced professional/community document for
acquiring, managing, preserving, and making zines accessible. It explicitly
says it is intended to prompt questions rather than supply definitive answers,
is not prescriptive or absolute, and should change as community norms evolve.
That stance is itself important metadata guidance: a catalogue policy should
make decisions reviewable and revisable, not present institutional description
as neutral fact.

Its cataloguing guidance, as summarized by the community's cataloguing page,
raises creator privacy, the right to remain anonymous, the limits of standard
classification, deadnames and trans identity, author-supplied descriptions,
OCLC harvesting, and the distinction between what a cataloguer can discover and
what should be made public. It also discusses local fields, local subject
headings, content genre versus form genre, and the need to match description to
the users and purpose of a collection.

**Limitations:** This is authoritative community guidance but not a formal
library standard, peer-reviewed article, or legal statement. The site currently
notes that a revised edition is in progress; the 2015 edition should therefore
be versioned in any policy citation. It represents a collaborative zine-library
community with substantial US/English-language participation, not every zine
community.

**Zine Librarians cataloguing resource page, "zine cataloging."**
[https://www.zinelibraries.info/running-a-zine-library/zine-cataloging/](https://www.zinelibraries.info/running-a-zine-library/zine-cataloging/)

This page is a useful bibliography and practice index. It points to O'Dell,
Freedman and Kauffman, Berthoud, the Queer Zine Library manual, ZAPP
cataloguing instructions, Franklin & Marshall procedures, Anchor Archive
subjects, the Art & Architecture Thesaurus, and examples from Barnard, Reed,
Los Angeles Public Library, and Michigan State. The page records a 2016
cataloguing session in which practitioners discussed:

- adapting local MARC fields when national best practices are incomplete;
- privacy risks of OCLC records and search-engine harvesting;
- author-supplied descriptions and consent;
- current versus historical publication contexts;
- deadnames and creator-chosen identity;
- using local subject fields while waiting for broader standards;
- not shelving by subject when a zine spans many topics;
- distinguishing content genre from form genre; and
- cataloguing/shelving choices according to the intended users.

**Limitations:** This is a curated web page of notes and links, not a
systematic review. Some linked resources are presentations, local procedures,
or no-longer-maintained sites. The page should be used to locate primary
artifacts, not as evidence that every linked practice is current or widely
validated.

**Zine Union Catalog / ZineCat overview.**
[https://www.zinelibraries.info/zine-union-catalog/](https://www.zinelibraries.info/zine-union-catalog/)

ZineCat is described as a union catalogue intended to share cataloguing and
holdings information across libraries with disparate metadata schemas and
controlled or uncontrolled vocabularies. Its stated goal is a Linked Open Data
catalogue usable by both large research institutions and small independent
libraries. The page also describes xZINECOREx as a Dublin Core-based starting
point for shared cataloguing.

This is a concrete example of interoperability as a community infrastructure
problem. A shared catalogue needs common fields, but it also needs to accept
records made by institutions with different resources and descriptive norms.
That supports a crosswalk/export strategy rather than requiring one local
catalogue model to encode every community's practice.

**Limitations:** The page describes a work in progress and a project history,
not a current normative schema specification. The operational status, field
definitions, and governance of the live service should be checked separately.
The earlier xZINECOREx description is a baseline, not evidence that Dublin Core
alone preserves all zine-specific meaning.

**Franklin & Marshall College, "Cataloging Zines: FM Zine Library
Procedures" (September 2019 version).**
[PDF](https://www.zinelibraries.info/wp-content/uploads/2022/10/Cataloging-Zines_-FM-Zine-Library-Procedures.pdf)

This is a useful local technical procedure because it documents an institution's
actual operational choices rather than an abstract standard. It belongs in the
evidence set for comparing minimum intake fields, name treatment, physical
description, subjects, notes, and workflow.

**Limitations:** It is a local procedure from a college collection and a 2019
version. It is not a universal rule, and its fields may be shaped by the
institution's cataloguing system, staffing, collection scope, and users. The
PDF should be read directly before importing any field-level rule; this review
does not claim that every local instruction is suitable for Biblioteca.

**Queer Zine Library cataloguing manual.** The Zine Librarians cataloguing page
links to the manual shared by the London-based roaming DIY queer zine library:
[Queer Zine Library](https://docs.google.com/document/d/12KluusaAdw7Ao5cYXK6sxA1Okm-0anghvWVpVb7fwjs/edit).

Its importance is methodological: a community-defined collection can document
its own cataloguing choices, rather than being described only through an
external library's authority files. The manual should be compared with formal
RDA/MARC practice for creator names, terms, notes, and physical features.

**Limitations:** The linked Google document was not reliably retrievable in the
research environment, and its version/history and authorship need verification
before citation as a stable policy. The collection is explicitly community and
queer-zine focused; it should not be generalized without consultation.

### Institutional collection guidance

**Barnard Zine Library, About the collection.**
[https://zines.barnard.edu/](https://zines.barnard.edu/)

Barnard's official collection page states that the library acquires zines by
women, transfeminine people, trans women, and trans men, while welcoming any
time period or subject. It describes a broad range of personal and political
topics and invites visitors and classes by advance arrangement. The collection
scope demonstrates that identity, community, and subject scope can be part of a
collection's public rationale, not hidden assumptions in a catalogue record.

The site's current search examples also show a practical subject/form tension:
Barnard directs users to search its union catalogue with a zine genre/form
facet, location, and topical terms, and documents false hits when a broad term
is searched. This supports keeping publication form, local holding/location,
and topic as separate dimensions.

**Limitations:** A collection home page is not a cataloguing manual. Barnard's
scope is institution-specific, identity-focused, and historically situated; it
does not prescribe how every zine library should define gender, identity,
subject, or access. Current pages may change, so capture the access date and
page version when relying on them.

**Barnard collection access page and catalogue examples.** The same
institutional site provides access information and links to its Columbia
catalogue records. These are useful for studying the relationship between a
public collection description, location/holding data, and a union catalogue
record, but they should be treated as examples rather than a benchmark for
record quality.

## Tensions and design implications

### 1. Creator identity, anonymity, pseudonyms, and collectives

Standard authority control favors a stable authorized form of name. Zines often
favor a chosen name, pseudonym, collective, imprint, initials, no name, or a
name that changes over time. The zine-specific guidance treats privacy and the
right to remain anonymous as substantive concerns, not cataloguer errors. It
also warns that author-supplied material is not automatically permission to
republish it and that trans creators can be harmed by deadnames or historical
identity research.

Recommended local model:

- Store the public display name exactly as the creator or publication presents
  it, with a source note.
- Distinguish identified, pseudonymous, anonymous, unknown, and unresolved
  authorship. Do not turn "anonymous" into a guessed legal person.
- Treat a collective as an agent in its own right. Do not split a collective
  into individual members unless the publication or a consented source makes
  that relationship public and useful.
- Keep legal name, contact information, inferred identity, and sensitive
  biography out of the public catalogue by default.
- Put creator, editor, illustrator, publisher, distributor, submitter, and
  archive steward on contextual role assertions. A person can have several
  roles, and the same name does not prove that the roles are identical.
- Preserve a creator's chosen spelling, diacritics, capitalization, and
  pronouns where the system can do so, while allowing a controlled search
  access point as a separate value.

Synthetic example: a zine credits `Night Bus` and includes no legal name. The
public record should credit `Night Bus` as the asserted creator and mark the
authorship as pseudonymous or unidentified according to the evidence. It should
not add a guessed personal name because a cataloguer recognizes the writing.

### 2. Community language and creator autonomy

Community terms can be both discovery tools and identity claims. Replacing a
creator's term with a library-preferred term can improve cross-collection
retrieval while changing the publication's voice or applying an outsider's
classification. The Code of Ethics' deliberately non-prescriptive stance and
the existence of community-authored manuals support an explicit provenance
distinction:

- creator-supplied statement;
- cataloguer transcription or summary;
- controlled subject/form mapping;
- community review or correction; and
- public display decision.

A local catalogue should retain the original wording even when it also adds a
controlled term. Corrections should be additive or versioned where possible,
not silent overwrites. A creator should be able to request correction,
suppression, or a change in display without having to prove a legal identity.

Synthetic example: a maker describes a publication as a `diaspora kitchen
newspaper`. Retain that phrase in a note or description, and optionally map it
to a governed topical term. Do not replace it with only `cookery` or `ethnic
relations`.

### 3. Subject versus genre/form

The sources consistently support separating what a publication is from what it
is about. `Zine`, `perzine`, `fanzine`, `newsletter`, `comic`, or `single-sheet`
can describe form or genre. `Punk`, `housing`, `trans health`, or a named place
can describe subject or coverage. The cataloguing notes warn that zines often
contain many subjects and that shelving by one subject can reduce the work.
Barnard's examples combine a zine genre/form facet with topical searching.

Recommended local model:

- Keep `form/genre`, `subject`, and `physical/delivery format` distinct.
- Allow multiple subjects and multiple forms without pretending that array
  order expresses importance.
- Store vocabulary identifier, label, version, source, and local mapping.
- Preserve creator language and free-text summary separately from governed terms.
- Avoid treating a local category as an authoritative thesaurus term until its
  scope, preferred label, aliases, and governance are documented.
- Do not equate a collection's shelving categories with the meaning of the
  publication.

The Anchor Archive subject thesaurus is linked by the zine librarians' page as
a community-oriented controlled vocabulary, while the Art & Architecture
Thesaurus is suggested for material and production features. These are useful
candidate vocabularies, not complete or neutral coverage of every language,
community, or political context.

### 4. Physical description and material specificity

Zines make physical description unusually meaningful: folded sheets, hand
stapling, photocopy, risograph, collage, altered books, inserts, loose pages,
handwritten text, and unconventional pagination can be part of the publication's
meaning and use. O'Dell's abstract specifically identifies object cataloguing
and blurred work/expression/manifestation boundaries as issues.

Recommended local model:

- Separate the publication's described form from the particular copy observed.
- Record extent, dimensions, binding/assembly, reproduction method, color, and
  notable material features when they aid identification, preservation, or use.
- Preserve an uncertain or creator-supplied physical statement rather than
  inventing conventional pagination.
- Do not infer physical characteristics from a PDF, cover image, or filename.
- Keep digital file page count, scan dimensions, file format, checksum, and
  derivative relationship on the asset/version, not on the physical
  publication record.
- Use a free-text note for materially important features that a controlled
  vocabulary cannot express.

Synthetic example: `12 folded sheets, handwritten pagination, two loose inserts`
is a physical observation. It should not be normalized to `24 pages` without
also preserving the observation, because the assembly itself may matter.

### 5. Date, place, and local context

Irregular frequency, undated issues, seasonal dates, reprints, and community
events make a single normalized publication date inadequate. Place is similarly
role-dependent: a zine may be made in one place, printed in another, circulated
at a third, and held by a fourth. A city in a creator biography is not
automatically a publication place.

Recommended local model:

- Preserve the source/display statement (`winter 2011`, `circa 2004`, or
  `undated`) alongside a normalized date or interval when defensible.
- Label date roles such as publication, creation, revision, acquisition, scan,
  or deposit.
- Record place with a role: place of creation, publication, distribution,
  circulation, event, creator location, or holding location.
- Keep the evidence and confidence for inferred dates or places.
- Do not infer a place from the archive's own location or from a creator's
  current address.
- Treat local context as discoverable metadata only when it is appropriate to
  publish. A neighborhood, venue, or mutual-aid network may be sensitive.

This approach aligns with the collection-policy guidance, which asks libraries
to state geographic scope, dates, languages, audience, and community profile,
while preventing those collection-level facts from being mistaken for facts
about every publication.

### 6. Holdings, custody, and digital files

Zine union-catalog practice makes holdings shareable, but a holding is not the
publication itself. A repository may own one copy, have a loanable copy, link
to a creator-authorized digital file, or merely have found a URL. These are
different assertions.

Recommended local model:

- Separate publication, edition/issue, repository holding, physical copy, file
  asset/version, and derivative.
- Record repository, shelfmark, copy count, condition, and local availability
  on the holding or copy.
- Record URL, file format, checksum, scan properties, source, and derivative
  relationship on the asset/version.
- Distinguish discovery, reading, download, preservation, replication, and
  reuse access decisions.
- Do not treat a public URL as proof of custody, authenticity, creator consent,
  or copyright permission.
- Preserve ambiguous matches between catalogue records and local files as
  unresolved evidence, not as confirmed identity.

Synthetic example: Biblioteca has a local PDF named `signal-fold.pdf` and a
catalogue record for a similarly titled printed issue. The file can be tracked
as an ambiguous local archive file until title, issue, pages, provenance, or
creator evidence establishes the match.

### 7. Rights, privacy, and access

Zine practice often includes explicit copying invitations, anti-copyright
statements, or informal permissions. Those statements matter, but they are not
always legal licenses and may apply differently to physical duplication,
digitization, public display, download, derivative creation, or preservation.
The Code of Ethics and cataloguing notes also show that privacy can conflict
with the discovery benefits of a union catalogue or web harvesting.

Recommended local model:

- Preserve the original rights or copying statement verbatim and identify its
  source.
- Separate legal rights claim, creator copying request, repository permission,
  and operational access decision.
- Scope each decision to a publication, issue, copy, file, page, or derivative.
- Record who made the assertion, when, under what evidence, and with what
  visibility.
- Treat unknown rights as unknown, not as permission.
- Allow discovery without necessarily allowing reading, downloading,
  preservation, replication, or reuse.
- Provide a contact or correction route that does not require public disclosure
  of a pseudonymous creator's identity.
- Consider delayed indexing, restricted notes, no-search display, or takedown/
  correction workflows when public metadata itself creates harm.

Synthetic example: a cover says `please copy, do not sell`, while the submitter
has not granted permission to digitize the physical copy. Record both the
creator statement and the Biblioteca digitization decision; do not convert the
cover phrase into an unrestricted digital license.

## Practical field-level baseline

The following is a research-informed baseline, not a replacement for the local
domain model or a formal cataloguing code.

| Area | Preserve | Avoid |
| --- | --- | --- |
| Title | title as presented, normalized search title if needed, alternate titles | silently regularizing expressive spelling |
| Responsibility | public name, pseudonym, collective, contextual role, source, authorship status | guessing legal identity or treating submitter as creator |
| Description | creator statement, cataloguer summary, language, source, visibility | overwriting community language with a single official phrase |
| Subject/form | separate repeatable terms, vocabulary/version, local mapping | one mixed category field |
| Date | source statement, role, normalized interval, precision, uncertainty | forcing an exact year |
| Place | place plus role and evidence | using one undifferentiated place field |
| Physical | extent, dimensions, binding, production method, notable features | inferring physical facts from digital assets |
| Holdings | repository, copy, location, condition, call number, custody evidence | putting holding status on the publication itself |
| Files | asset kind, source, format, checksum, derivative relation, access scope | treating any URL as an original or authorization |
| Rights | original statement, claimant/source, action, scope, date, confidence | treating `unknown` or `public` as a license |
| Privacy | field/relationship visibility, consent, correction/suppression history | one public/private switch for all data |

## Recommended operating principles for Biblioteca

1. Make a public catalogue projection from richer internal assertions. The
   public projection should not be the only place where provenance, uncertainty,
   or privacy decisions exist.
2. Keep creator-supplied wording and controlled mappings side by side.
3. Model agents and role assertions separately from publications.
4. Keep anonymous, pseudonymous, unknown, private, and unresolved distinct.
5. Treat local context and place as role-bearing claims with appropriate
   visibility.
6. Separate publication description, holding, physical copy, and digital asset.
7. Preserve physical observations even when no standard vocabulary term fits.
8. Keep legal rights, copying requests, and access decisions separate.
9. Version local vocabularies and retain historical terms/mappings.
10. Provide creator correction and withdrawal paths as catalogue functionality,
    not only as informal email support.
11. Use standards and union-catalogue exports as interoperability layers, with
    documented information loss.
12. Review policies with the communities represented by the collection, rather
    than treating community consultation as a one-time intake step.

## Synthetic test cases for future crosswalk work

These cases are deliberately invented and should not be mistaken for source
records.

- `Paper Moon`, credited to the collective `Kitchen Table Radio`, no individual
  names, issue stated as `late autumn`; test collective identity, uncertain date,
  and creator wording.
- `Street Signals`, signed `M.` with a creator request not to connect the name
  to an offline identity; test pseudonym, public/private projections, and
  correction.
- `After the Flood`, a photocopied, hand-stapled zine with loose inserts and a
  cover statement `copy freely`; test physical description and separate rights
  and digitization decisions.
- `Small Rooms`, a bilingual issue with a local term for its community that has
  no direct controlled-vocabulary equivalent; test language-tagged creator text,
  local mapping, and subject/form separation.
- `Signal Fold`, a printed issue held physically by Biblioteca and represented
  online by a scan from an unknown source; test holding, asset provenance,
  ambiguous match, and independent reading/download permissions.

## Overall limitations

- The zine-specific literature is relatively small and is concentrated in
  English-language, US/UK library contexts. The Brazilian thesis above helps
  widen the context but does not by itself establish a Latin American norm.
- Several highly useful sources are practitioner reflections, community pages,
  manuals, or local procedures rather than peer-reviewed research.
- Paywalls and bot protection limited direct access to some full texts. Claims
  attributed to abstracts or landing pages are intentionally narrower than
  claims that would require reading the full work.
- Institutional collection pages show local choices, not universal best
  practice. They can also change without preserving a public version history.
- Community guidance is living and contested. The 2015 Code of Ethics is
  explicitly under revision, so its edition/date must be cited.
- No source reviewed resolves the legal status of every zine copying statement,
  the privacy implications of every name authority decision, or the governance
  of every community vocabulary.
- Standards and linked-data models can improve interoperability while still
  losing tone, context, consent, uncertainty, and power relationships. Any
  export should document those losses and retain the richer local evidence.
